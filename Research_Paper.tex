\begin{abstract}
We investigate cross-attack generalisation of adversarially augmented
DistilBERT-based AI text detection at consumer-GPU scale. A binary
classifier is fine-tuned on the DetectRL benchmark using character-level
attack types (homoglyph substitution, word substitution) and evaluated on
mechanistically distinct semantic-level unseen attacks (paraphrase,
prompt-based adversarial) withheld entirely from training. All four
ablation configurations achieve Relative Robustness Degradation values
between 1.1\% and 3.1\%, with unseen-split F1 ranging from 0.913 to
0.927---well within the 5\% generalisation threshold---providing a positive
empirical answer to whether adversarial augmentation generalises across the
character-level/semantic-level attack divide at 66M parameter scale. An
ablation study reveals that full encoder fine-tuning improves both
in-distribution performance and cross-attack generalisation simultaneously,
while deeper classification heads modestly trade generalisation for
seen-set precision. Four parallel and distributed computing optimisations
are implemented and benchmarked on a single RTX~3050 8~GB GPU: parallel
preprocessing (3.58x speedup), optimised DataLoader pipeline, AMP training
with enlarged batch size (3.32x speedup), and AMP inference mode (54.25\%
latency reduction, 380.64 samples/second). As auxiliary contributions,
Fast-DetectGPT is reproduced at AUROC 0.937 and extended with 4-bit NF4
quantization on RAID adversarial subsets, establishing that
clean-benchmark performance degrades sharply under adversarial attack
(AUROC 0.669--0.789), with homoglyph substitution substantially more
destructive than paraphrase rewriting (gpt2-xl RRD: 50.12\%).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 and LLaMA make fluent, human-sounding
text trivially producible at scale. This capability threatens domains dependent
on content authenticity, particularly academia and journalism, where machine-generated
submissions bypass standard verification. Human inspection fails; research shows
people detect AI-generated text at rates barely above chance~\cite{ippolito2020}.
Automated detection is therefore the only scalable response.

Two dominant paradigms have emerged for automated detection.
\textbf{Zero-shot probability-based methods} such as Fast-DetectGPT~\cite{bao2024}
and Binoculars~\cite{hans2024} require no labelled training data. They measure
whether a text occupies a high-probability region of an LLM's output distribution,
achieving AUROC values exceeding 0.98 on clean benchmarks. However, the RAID
benchmark~\cite{dugan2024} (an adversarial evaluation spanning 6.2~million samples,
11~LLMs, and 11~attacks) showed this class of detector is fragile. Homoglyph
substitution alone caused accuracy drops of up to 40.6\% for specific detectors.
The vulnerability is architectural. Any surface-level manipulation that shifts
token log-probabilities directly disrupts the curvature-based detection signal.

\textbf{Supervised fine-tuning} addresses this fragility by hypothesis: a
model trained on labelled human and AI text learns stylistic representations
distributed across encoder weights instead of relying on surface probability
statistics. Such representations are harder to fool through adversarial
token-level perturbation. However, Huang et al.~\cite{huang2024} challenged
this assumption. They found that training on adversarially augmented data does
not automatically generalise robustness to unseen attack types, and may actively
worsen cross-attack robustness relative to a clean-trained baseline. This
\emph{cross-attack generalisation gap}---whether supervised adversarial training
on one class of attacks produces models robust to a mechanistically distinct
class---remains open in the literature.

We investigate whether a DistilBERT-based~\cite{sanh2019} binary text
classifier, fine-tuned on a stratified subset of the DetectRL
benchmark~\cite{wu2024detectrl}, generalises robustness from character-level
attacks seen during training to semantic-level attacks withheld from
training. The experimental design is conservative: we train on homoglyph
and word substitution attacks (character-level mechanisms) and evaluate
on paraphrase and prompt-based adversarial attacks (semantic-level
mechanisms). These two classes are mechanistically distinct, providing a
strict test of the generalisation hypothesis. Whether generalisation holds,
fails, or worsens performance relative to a clean baseline, the outcome
remains scientifically informative.

We select DistilBERT at 66~million parameters for two reasons. Practically,
it is the most capable model deployable within the 8~GB VRAM constraint of
the experimental hardware. Scientifically, verifying whether the cross-attack
generalisation property exists at compressed parameter scale is an independent
contribution. Larger adversarially robust models in the literature (e.g.,
GREATER by Li et al.~\cite{li2025}) require hardware inaccessible in
consumer-grade settings.

The systems contribution of this paper addresses the engineering required to
train four ablation configurations on 120,000~samples over three epochs on a
single consumer GPU. We implement and evaluate four parallel and distributed
computing (PDC) optimisations: parallel multi-stage dataset preprocessing (TC1),
an optimised DataLoader pipeline (TC2), Automatic Mixed Precision training (TC3),
and AMP-combined inference-mode acceleration (TC4). We benchmark these
optimisations against sequential baselines to quantify efficiency gains.

This paper also integrates two auxiliary empirical contributions against a
Fast-DetectGPT baseline. First, we reproduced the original paper's
ChatGPT/GPT-4 aggregate AUROC (0.937 vs.\ the paper's 0.933 target) to
confirm implementation fidelity. Second, we extended Fast-DetectGPT with
4-bit NF4 quantization and evaluated it on RAID homoglyph and paraphrase
subsets. This represents the first adversarial characterisation of this
detector under the RAID benchmark's specific attack conditions.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Research Questions.} This paper addresses two primary
questions:

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ1:} Does a DistilBERT model trained on character-level
adversarial attack types from DetectRL generalise robustness to
semantically distinct unseen attack types, and does this cross-attack
generalisation exceed that of zero-shot probabilistic detectors under the
same conditions?

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ2:} How do classification head depth and encoder
fine-tuning depth independently affect clean detection accuracy and
cross-attack adversarial robustness?

\smallskip
The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:bg}
reviews relevant background and prior systems. Section~\ref{sec:model}
describes the proposed model and its design choices.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the experimental setup, datasets, and PDC
optimisation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents reproduced

Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses findings, limitations, and
implications. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Background}\label{sec:bg}

\subsection{Methodological Generations in AI Text Detection}

Detecting machine-generated text is a specialised instance of authorship
classification, a foundational problem in natural language processing.
Four methodological generations exist in the literature.

\textbf{First generation---statistical features.} Early systems used
hand-crafted statistical signals: perplexity ratios, log-rank statistics,
and TF-IDF representations fed into logistic regression or support vector
machines~\cite{jawahar2020}. These interpretable approaches fail to capture
the compositional, high-dimensional structure separating human from AI
writing~\cite{goodfellow2016}. They collapse under mild adversarial perturbation.

\textbf{Second generation---probability curvature.} DetectGPT~\cite{mitchell2023}
introduced zero-shot detection via probability curvature analysis.
Machine-generated text tends to occupy local maxima of a language model's
log-probability surface, while human text does not. The detection signal
is the difference between a passage's log-probability and the expected
log-probability of its perturbed variants. This required approximately
100~perturbations per sample, making it computationally prohibitive at scale.

\textbf{Third generation---efficient zero-shot detection.}
Fast-DetectGPT~\cite{bao2024} replaced expensive perturbation sampling
with conditional probability curvature computed analytically. This achieves
approximately a 340$\times$ speedup over DetectGPT while maintaining or
improving AUROC. Binoculars~\cite{hans2024} takes a different approach:
using the ratio of perplexity under an observer LLM to cross-perplexity
between two LLMs (Falcon-7B Instruct and Falcon-7B), it achieves AUROC
consistently above 0.99 on clean benchmarks at a 0.01\% false positive rate.
The RAID benchmark~\cite{dugan2024} subsequently stress-tested the entire
third generation at scale. It revealed the structural vulnerability of
probability-based signals: character-level perturbations that alter tokenisation
cause AUROC drops of up to 40.6\% for specific detectors, as the attack
disrupts the token-probability surface required for the detection signal.

\textbf{Fourth generation---adversarially augmented supervised training.}
RADAR~\cite{hu2023} proposed jointly training a paraphraser and a
detector in an adversarial loop, improving robustness to paraphrase attacks.
Li et al.~\cite{li2025} (GREATER, ACL 2025) advanced this via greedy
token-level perturbation in embedding space and synchronous adversary-detector
updating, achieving current state-of-the-art performance. However, Huang et
al.~\cite{huang2024} identified a critical open problem: training on
adversarially augmented data does not automatically generalise robustness
across unseen attack types. In some configurations, it actively worsens
cross-attack robustness relative to a clean-trained baseline. GREATER's
analysis explicitly flags the cross-attack generalisation gap as unsolved
even at its scale. This paper targets that gap.

As detection methodology evolved, evaluation benchmarks grew correspondingly
more rigorous. Wu et al.~\cite{wu2025survey} survey these directions, from
statistical heuristics to adversarially robust deep representation learning.

\subsection{Primary Baselines}

\subsubsection{RAID RoBERTa (Dugan et al., ACL 2024)}

RAID~\cite{dugan2024} is the largest AI text detection benchmark to date:
6.2~million samples across 11~LLMs, 8~domains, 11~adversarial attacks, and
4~decoding strategies. It evaluates 12~existing detectors at a fixed 5\%
false positive rate. Relevant to this work, RAID shows that accuracy
differences between detectors under adversarial conditions are far larger
than differences under clean conditions, making adversarial robustness the
superior discriminating evaluation criterion. Furthermore, homoglyph attacks
caused accuracy drops of up to 40.6\% for specific detectors, while GPTZero
dropped only 0.3\% under the same attack, proving that adversarial
vulnerability is highly detector-specific.

Crucially, RAID evaluates off-the-shelf systems rather than training new
models. It does not study whether training-time exposure to specific attack
types generalises robustness to others. RAID's 6.2~million-sample scale also
makes it impractical as a training corpus for single-GPU consumer experiments.
Therefore, DetectRL~\cite{wu2024detectrl}, with a cleaner attack taxonomy
and approximately 235,200~samples, serves as the training resource for this
study.

\subsubsection{Fast-DetectGPT (Bao et al., ICLR 2024)}

Fast-DetectGPT~\cite{bao2024} is a fully zero-shot detector requiring no
labelled training data. For a passage $x$, it computes the conditional
probability curvature score:
\begin{equation}
d(x) = \log p_\theta(x) - \mathbb{E}_{x' \sim q(\cdot|x)}[\log p_\theta(x')]
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where $q(\cdot|x)$ is a conditional sampling distribution and $p_\theta$
is the scoring model. Passages with high $d(x)$ are classified as
machine-generated. The authors report AUROC of 0.9887 on 5-model
generations and 0.9338 on ChatGPT/GPT-4 generations. The fundamental
limitation of this approach is that the detection signal is entirely
probabilistic: any surface manipulation that shifts token log-probabilities
directly degrades it. We reproduce this baseline in Section~\ref{ssec:reproduced}
and extend it adversarially in Section~\ref{ssec:additional}.

\subsubsection{Binoculars (Hans et al., ICML 2024)}

Binoculars~\cite{hans2024} uses two Falcon-7B variants (observer and
performer) to compute a ratio of perplexity to cross-perplexity, achieving
AUROC above 0.99 on clean benchmarks. Running two 7B-parameter models
simultaneously requires approximately 28~GB VRAM, precluding local
evaluation on the experimental hardware (RTX~3050, 8~GB). We report
paper-stated Binoculars results as a reference point, treating it as a
ceiling benchmark for zero-shot methods under clean conditions.

\subsection{Additional Related Work}

\textbf{Detection problem foundations.} Ippolito et al.~\cite{ippolito2020}
established that automated detection is most effective when humans
fail, providing the central motivation for automated approaches. Their core
finding---that fine-tuned neural models consistently outperform statistical
baselines given labelled data---directly supports the supervised paradigm
used here.

\textbf{Pre-trained encoder foundations.} Devlin et al.~\cite{devlin2019}
(BERT) established bidirectional transformer pre-training via masked language
modelling. DistilBERT~\cite{sanh2019} distils BERT into a 66M-parameter model,
retaining 97\% of BERT's language understanding capability at 60\% of its size.
This makes it the appropriate choice given the 8~GB VRAM budget.
DeBERTa~\cite{he2021} improves on BERT through disentangled attention, but
its 183M--750M parameter scale exceeds the hardware budget, serving as the
upper-end reference point for model selection.

\textbf{Adversarial training context.} Kirchenbauer et al.~\cite{kirchenbauer2023}
introduced watermark-based detection. However, watermarking requires cooperation
from the generating system, placing it outside the threat model addressed here.
RADAR~\cite{hu2023} is the first adversarial training approach specifically for
AI text detection. It demonstrates paraphrase robustness gains through
adversary-detector co-training, though restricted to a single attack type.
This paper extends that question to multi-attack-type training with cross-type
evaluation. GREATER~\cite{li2025} is the current state of the art, but requires
hardware inaccessible in consumer-grade settings.

\textbf{The cross-attack generalisation problem.} Huang et al.~\cite{huang2024}
demonstrated empirically that adversarially augmented training does not
automatically generalise and may cause active harm. The controlled experimental
design of this paper---training on character-level attacks and evaluating on
semantic-level attacks---is calibrated to produce an interpretable result
relative to Huang et al.'s findings, whether positive (generalisation holds),
negative (fails), or adverse (performance worse than clean baseline).

\subsection{Datasets}

\textbf{DetectRL}~\cite{wu2024detectrl} is the primary training and
seen-test dataset. It contains approximately 235,200~labelled samples
across clean human-written and AI-generated text, alongside multiple
adversarially perturbed variants. Attack types are organised into separable
categories: direct prompt (clean AI), homoglyph substitution, word
substitution, paraphrase attacks, prompt-based adversarial attacks, data
mixing, and spelling errors. This categorisation allows the controlled
seen/unseen split central to this study. Spelling error and data mixing
subsets are excluded from both training and evaluation. Spelling errors
create ground-truth ambiguity with naturally occurring typos, and data mixing
samples (mixed human-AI content) possess no unambiguous binary label.

\textbf{RAID}~\cite{dugan2024} serves as the adversarial evaluation corpus
for the Fast-DetectGPT extension experiment (Section~\ref{ssec:additional}).
From the RAID training split, we extract two attack-type subsets: homoglyph
substitution (character-level Unicode lookalike replacement) and paraphrase
rewriting (semantic-preserving surface alteration). Each subset is balanced
to 1,000~samples (500~human, 500~AI) using a fixed random seed of 42.

\section{Proposed Model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Core Design Philosophy}

The proposed model is a DistilBERT-based binary text classifier fine-tuned
on adversarially stratified training data. The design rests on three principles:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pre-trained encoder:} DistilBERT's six transformer layers already
encode rich syntactic and semantic representations from large corpora. Fine-tuning
adapts these features rather than learning from scratch on a relatively small
labelled dataset.
\item \textbf{Mechanistically diverse training data:} The training distribution
includes multiple adversarial attack types from the same mechanistic family. Exposing
the model to character-level perturbation variants (homoglyph and word substitution)
forces it to learn representations independent of surface token statistics.
\item \textbf{Strict unseen evaluation:} The held-out evaluation set is mechanistically
distinct from the training attacks. Withholding all semantic-level attacks (paraphrase
and prompt-based adversarial) ensures the cross-attack generalisation evaluation tests
true transfer, not interpolation.
\end{itemize}

The proposed model configuration is \texttt{ablation\_b} (a deep classification head
with partial encoder freezing), selected following the ablation study detailed below.
We train and evaluate four configurations; \texttt{ablation\_b} achieves the highest
validation F1 alongside the best VRAM efficiency profile (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Model Architecture}\label{ssec:arch}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figure1_architecture.png}}
\caption{DistilBERT binary classifier architecture. The proposed model
stacks a classification head atop the frozen \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased}
encoder. The [CLS] token's final hidden state (768-d) is passed to either
the single-layer head (\texttt{Dropout $\rightarrow$ Linear(768$\rightarrow$2)})
or the deep head (\texttt{Dropout $\rightarrow$ Linear(768$\rightarrow$384)
$\rightarrow$ GELU $\rightarrow$ Dropout $\rightarrow$ Linear(384$\rightarrow$2)}).
Embedding layers are always frozen; transformer layers 0--3 are optionally
frozen depending on ablation configuration.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Encoder backbone.} The base model is \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased}~\cite{sanh2019},
featuring 6~transformer layers, a hidden dimension of 768, 12~attention heads per layer,
and a WordPiece vocabulary of 30,522~entries. The total parameter count is approximately
66~million. We freeze the embedding layer during fine-tuning across all four configurations,
excluding its parameters from gradient computation. The classification head receives the
[CLS] token's final hidden state (a 768-dimensional vector) as the sequence representation.

\textbf{Classification head---single-layer variant.} This variant is used in
\texttt{baseline1} and \texttt{ablation\_a}:
\begin{center}
\small\texttt{Dropout(p=0.3) $\rightarrow$ Linear(768 $\rightarrow$ 2)}
\end{center}

\textbf{Classification head---deep variant (proposed model).} This variant is used in
\texttt{ablation\_b} and \texttt{ablation\_c}:
\begin{center}
\small\texttt{Dropout(p=0.3) $\rightarrow$ Linear(768$\rightarrow$384)
$\rightarrow$ GELU $\rightarrow$ Dropout(p=0.2) $\rightarrow$ Linear(384$\rightarrow$2)}
\end{center}
The deep head adds one hidden layer with a GELU non-linearity and a second dropout stage.
The intermediate dimension of 384 creates a lightweight bottleneck at half the encoder's
hidden size. This additional non-linear projection provides the capacity to learn
attack-type-invariant decision boundaries from the encoder's representation, without
materially increasing parameter count or VRAM overhead relative to the encoder itself.

\textbf{Layer freezing strategy.} We study four configurations to isolate the independent
effects of head depth and encoder fine-tuning depth, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:configs}.

\begin{table}[t]

\textbf{Config} & \textbf{Head} & \textbf{Frozen Layers} & \textbf{Description}\\
\midrule

Freezing the lower four transformer layers preserves the general linguistic
representations learned during pre-training (syntactic and morphological);
the upper two layers are free to specialise toward the detection task.
This is a standard partial-freezing heuristic for fine-tuning on limited
data~\cite{goodfellow2016}, motivated by the constraint that lower layers
should not be overwritten by attack-specific signals that may not generalise.

\subsection{Seen / Unseen Attack Split}\label{ssec:split}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figure2_split_pipeline.png}}
\caption{Seen / Unseen attack split pipeline. The DetectRL corpus is
partitioned into a seen family (Direct Prompt, Homoglyph, Word
Substitution---used for training and seen-test evaluation) and an unseen
family (Paraphrase and Prompt-based Adversarial---held out entirely).
Spelling error and data-mixing subsets are excluded from both splits. The
seen/unseen boundary separates character-level mechanisms from
semantic-level mechanisms; the cross-attack evaluation is thereby strictly
conservative.}
\label{fig:split}
\end{figure*}

The central experimental manipulation is the stratified seen/unseen split
of DetectRL's attack taxonomy. The split maps DetectRL's internal
\texttt{data\_type} labels into three groups.

\textbf{Seen attack family.} This group comprises three DetectRL \texttt{data\_type}
categories: \texttt{direct\_prompt} (clean AI-generated text with no adversarial
modification), \texttt{adversarial\_character\_llm} (homoglyph substitution), and
\texttt{adversarial\_word\_llm} combined with \texttt{adversarial\_character\_word\_llm}
(word substitution). These categories form the training and seen-test distribution.

\textbf{Unseen attack family.} Withheld from training entirely and used solely for
held-out evaluation, this group comprises six DetectRL data types spanning two
semantic-level mechanisms. It includes paraphrase attacks (\texttt{paraphrase\_back\_translation\_llm},
\texttt{paraphrase\_polish\_llm}, \texttt{paraphrase\_dipper\_llm}) and prompt-based
adversarial attacks (\texttt{prompt\_few\_shot}, \texttt{prompt\_sico}).

\textbf{Excluded subsets.} Two data types are excluded from both training and evaluation.
Spelling error substitutions are excluded because they overlap with the natural typo
distribution of human-written text, creating ambiguous ground-truth labels that would
contaminate the classifier. Data mixing samples are excluded because their composite
human-AI authorship lacks an unambiguous binary label.

This split explicitly separates mechanisms: character-level (seen) versus semantic-level
(unseen) attacks. If generalisation holds across this boundary, it indicates true
attack-type-invariant representation learning. If it fails, the negative result remains
scientifically informative.

\subsection{Training Protocol}

\textbf{Dataset construction.} We undersample AI text from the DetectRL training
pool to equalise representation across the three seen attack groups. Combining
these with an equal number of human samples yields a balanced training pool of
120,000~samples (60,000~human, 60,000~AI). Random permutation with seed~42 splits
this pool 80/10/10 into 96,000~training samples, 12,000~validation samples, and
12,000~seen-test samples. We construct the unseen evaluation pool separately:
paraphrase and prompt-adversarial attack subsets are balanced to 20,000~samples
(10,000~human, 10,000~AI) and strictly withheld from training.

\textbf{Pre-tokenisation caching.} All samples are tokenised once at
\texttt{max\_length=256} with padding and truncation before training begins,
and stored as \texttt{.pt} tensor caches on disk. DataLoader workers load
pre-computed tensors directly, eliminating tokenisation overhead from the training
loop and raising GPU utilisation from approximately 30--50\% to 80--90\%+.
We truncate to 256 (rather than DistilBERT's maximum of 512) to permit
\texttt{batch\_size=32} within 8~GB VRAM.

\textbf{Optimiser and regularisation.} We train all configurations using the
AdamW optimiser at a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ and weight decay of 0.01,
minimising CrossEntropyLoss over two-class softmax outputs. Gradients are clipped
to a maximum L2~norm of 1.0 to prevent instability in early epochs. Each
configuration runs for 3~epochs. A global random seed of 42 is applied across
Python, NumPy, PyTorch, and CUDA for exact reproducibility.

\textbf{Checkpoint selection.} Validation F1 is computed post-epoch. The model
weights achieving the highest validation F1 across all epochs are saved and
used for all subsequent test and unseen-set evaluations.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

The primary evaluation metric is binary F1 score (macro-averaged). This guards
against the class-imbalance gaming that accuracy-based metrics suffer from.
ROC-AUC serves as a threshold-free measure of overall discriminative ability.
Accuracy, precision, and recall are reported as secondary diagnostics.
The primary cross-attack generalisation metric is Relative Robustness
Degradation (RRD):
\begin{equation}
\text{RRD}(\%) = \frac{F1_{\text{seen}} - F1_{\text{unseen}}}{F1_{\text{seen}}} \times 100
\label{eq:rrd}
\end{equation}
Lower RRD indicates superior cross-attack generalisation. An RRD below 5\%
indicates meaningful transfer of robustness; an RRD exceeding 10--15\% indicates
that generalisation has failed.

\subsection{PDC Systems Optimisations (TC1--TC4)}

Training four ablation configurations over 120,000~samples on a single RTX~3050 (8~GB)
requires deliberate systems engineering. We designed, implemented, and benchmarked
four optimisations:

\textbf{TC1---Parallel multi-stage preprocessing.} Dataset filtering and pool
construction are parallelised across 11 of the available 12~logical cores
(Ryzen~5600X) using Python's multiprocessing pool. Sequential filtering of the
full 744,912-row raw DetectRL corpus took 31.83~seconds; parallel filtering reduced
this to 8.89~seconds, a 3.58$\times$ speedup. The cached parquet workflow reduced
the total pipeline from $\sim$2.5~hours (initial download) to under 9~seconds
on subsequent runs.

\textbf{TC2---Optimised DataLoader pipeline.} The pre-tokenisation caching described
in Section~\ref{ssec:split} decouples tokenisation cost from the training loop.
Combined with \texttt{pin\_memory=True} (enabling asynchronous host-to-GPU transfers)
and \texttt{num\_workers=4} (safe on Windows when run standalone), GPU starvation
between batches is minimised. GPU VRAM utilisation during training sits at the
full 8~GB dedicated capacity.

\textbf{TC3---Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) training.} AMP training with
\texttt{GradScaler} is applied to \texttt{ablation\_b} (the proposed configuration)
with \texttt{batch\_size=128}---a 4$\times$ batch increase made feasible by the
reduced per-example VRAM footprint under FP16 activations. Under this regime,
\texttt{ablation\_b} completed 3~training epochs in 957.56~seconds at a peak VRAM
usage of 4,218.95~MB. Compared to the identical configuration under the baseline
32-batch training regime (3,180.47~seconds), this represents a 3.32$\times$
wall-clock speedup.

\textbf{TC4---AMP + inference-mode acceleration.} At inference time, combining
\texttt{torch.amp.autocast("cuda")} with \texttt{torch.inference\_mode()} eliminates
gradient bookkeeping. Benchmarked over 100~trials at batch size~32 with sequence
length~256, baseline FP32 latency was 183.75~ms/batch. The optimised configuration
achieved 84.07~ms/batch, representing a 54.25\% latency reduction and a throughput
of 380.64~samples/second.

\subsection{PDC Benchmarking Protocol}

Each PDC test case (TC1--TC4) is benchmarked in isolation against an
unoptimised sequential baseline, following the per-stage instrumentation
strategy of Isenko et al.~\cite{isenko2022}:

\textbf{TC1 benchmark.} We compare a sequential filter run (single process,
GIL-bound, timed from parquet load to processed pool save) against a parallel
filter run (11~workers via Python multiprocessing pool). Both runs process
the full 744,912-row raw corpus and produce identical output files. Speedup
is calculated as sequential\_time / parallel\_time.

\textbf{TC2 benchmark.} We measure GPU utilisation percentage via
\texttt{nvidia-smi} during training, comparing \texttt{num\_workers=0} against
\texttt{num\_workers=4} with \texttt{pin\_memory=True}. The pre-tokenisation
cache is active in both conditions to ensure the DataLoader's sole task is
tensor loading. This cleanly isolates the overlap benefit.

\textbf{TC3 benchmark.} We compare wall-clock training time for
\texttt{ablation\_b} (3~epochs, batch=128) using AMP (\texttt{GradScaler} +
\texttt{autocast}) against the identical configuration's baseline non-AMP
time at batch=32 from \texttt{training\_times.json}. We record peak VRAM via
\texttt{torch.cuda.max\_memory\_allocated()}. This comparison reflects both
the precision speedup and the larger batch size made possible by AMP, as
these factors operate in tandem.

\textbf{TC4 benchmark.} We conduct a 100-trial latency test (discarding a
10-trial warm-up) at batch=32 and seq\_len=256 using dummy input tensors.
The baseline is standard \texttt{torch.no\_grad()} FP32 inference. The
optimised condition uses \texttt{torch.inference\_mode()} combined with
\texttt{torch.amp.autocast("cuda")}. We report latency reduction percentage
and samples per second.

\textbf{Tokenizer compatibility patch.} The official Fast-DetectGPT
repository loads scoring-model tokenizers without configuring the padding
token. Decoder-only causal LLMs (like \texttt{gpt-j-6B} and \texttt{gpt2-xl})
do not define a \texttt{pad\_token} by default, causing a \texttt{ValueError}
when padding batched inputs. We applied two targeted fixes. First, a
\texttt{pad\_token is None} guard assigns the end-of-sequence token as the
padding sentinel (\texttt{tokenizer.pad\_token = tokenizer.eos\_token})---a
standard Hugging Face workaround for GPT-family models. Second, we explicitly
enforce right-padding (\texttt{tokenizer.padding\_side = "right"}) to guarantee
correct attention-mask alignment. Neither fix alters the scoring criterion.
The detection signal remains computed from per-token log-probabilities on the
non-padded prefix, while the attention mask correctly excludes padding positions
from the model's receptive field during inference.

the highest unseen F1 (0.9263), the highest unseen ROC-AUC (0.9790), and a

the model to develop representations more conducive to cross-attack transfer
than partial freezing allows.

The confusion matrices (Tables~\ref{tab:conf_seen} and \ref{tab:conf_unseen})
reveal a critical asymmetry across splits. On the seen split, the model
exhibits a strong recall bias: it misses only 114~AI samples (1.9\% of AI)
but misclassifies 539~human samples as AI (9.0\% of human). On the unseen
split, this asymmetry partially corrects. False negatives rise to 359 (7.2\%
of AI) and false positives fall to 378 (7.6\% of human), indicating the model
behaves more conservatively under out-of-distribution attack types. The
near-equal FN/FP balance on the unseen split (359 vs.\ 378) serves as a
positive generalisation signal, proving the decision boundary shifts
coherently rather than collapsing into a trivial class bias.

Training time scales entirely with the number of trainable parameters
rather than classification head depth. Fully unfrozen configurations
(\texttt{baseline1}, \texttt{ablation\_c}) require approximately 5,294~seconds.
Partially frozen configurations (\texttt{ablation\_a}, \texttt{ablation\_b})
require approximately 3,182~seconds. The four additional unfrozen transformer
layers drive this 1.66$\times$ overhead.

Homoglyph attacks prove substantially more destructive than paraphrase
attacks for both scoring models ($p \approx 0.000$ in both independent

near-random performance under homoglyph attack (AUROC 0.5257), whereas
\texttt{gpt-j-6B} retains moderate discriminative ability (AUROC 0.6689).
The \texttt{gpt2-xl} RRD of 50.12\% demonstrates that attack type---rather
than quantization level, scoring model size, or sample domain---serves as
the dominant determinant of detection difficulty under adversarial conditions.

Scoring model size provides homoglyph-specific robustness that fails to
appear under paraphrase conditions. On the paraphrase split, \texttt{gpt2-xl}
and \texttt{gpt-j-6B} perform nearly identically (AUROC 0.7893 vs.\ 0.7869,
a difference of 0.24~pp). On the homoglyph split, this gap widens to 14.3~pp.
The larger model's richer vocabulary representations retain discriminative
signal even when individual tokens suffer Unicode substitution corruption.

The 4-bit NF4 quantization delivers a measured 3.28$\times$ inference speedup
on the paraphrase subset (0.818~s/sample versus the 2.690~s/sample
reproduction baseline). Homoglyph runs execute more slowly at approximately
2.3~s/sample. Unicode character substitutions increase effective token
sequence complexity, partially offsetting the memory bandwidth savings
derived from quantization. VRAM reduction relative to FP16 loading is
estimated at approximately 75\% for \texttt{gpt-j-6B} ($\sim$14.0~GiB
$\rightarrow$ $\sim$3.5~GiB) and 70\% for \texttt{gpt2-xl} ($\sim$6.0~GiB
$\rightarrow$ $\sim$1.8~GiB).

Fast-DetectGPT's strong clean-benchmark performance does not transfer to
adversarially attacked text, regardless of quantization efficiency.
Adversarial training or attack-aware preprocessing remains a necessary
requirement for detection pipelines deployed in real-world scenarios.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Cross-Attack Generalisation: Answering RQ1}\label{ssec:rq1}

The results provide a clear, positive answer to RQ1: \emph{cross-attack
generalisation holds at the DistilBERT 66M parameter scale}. All four
ablation configurations achieve RRD values between 1.1\% and 3.1\%, remaining
well within the interpretive threshold of 5\% proposed in \eqref{eq:rrd}.
Unseen-split F1 values ranging from 0.913 to 0.927 demonstrate strong
performance on attack types never encountered during training. This confirms
that representations learned from character-level perturbation attacks
(homoglyph and word substitution) transfer meaningfully to semantic-level
unseen attacks (paraphrase and prompt-based adversarial). Addressing the
second part of RQ1, DistilBERT's unseen-split performance (F1 0.913--0.927)
substantially exceeds Fast-DetectGPT's adversarial AUROC under the identical
attack types (homoglyph: 0.526--0.669; paraphrase: 0.787--0.789). This
confirms that adversarially augmented supervised training proves more robust
to these attack conditions than zero-shot probability-based detection.

This outcome represents the positive generalisation scenario outlined in
Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Critically, it does not replicate the active-harm
pattern identified by Huang et al.~\cite{huang2024}, who found that
adversarially augmented training can sometimes worsen cross-attack robustness
relative to a clean-trained baseline. Multiple factors likely prevent the
active-harm pattern from emerging here. The seen attack types (homoglyph and
word substitution) are mechanistically diverse despite sharing a character-level
classification, which likely discourages over-fitting to any single surface
signature. Furthermore, DetectRL's balanced training composition prevents
any single attack type from dominating the learned representations. Finally,
because the unseen evaluation attacks are mechanistically distinct from both
training attack types, the risk of over-specialised representations interfering
with generalisation is reduced. Whether this positive transfer holds under
more aggressive, single-attack training compositions remains an open question.

The generalisation performance remains notably strong even for \texttt{baseline1},
the configuration with no layer freezing and a single-layer head (RRD 1.11\%,
unseen F1 0.9135). The base DistilBERT encoder, fine-tuned with character-level
adversarial data, evidently captures sufficient stylistic representations to
transfer across the seen/unseen attack divide independently of head complexity
or freezing strategy. The ablation study proves that architectural choices
modulate the \emph{degree} of generalisation, not its fundamental existence.

\subsection{Architectural Variable Effects: Answering RQ2}\label{ssec:rq2}

RQ2 investigates how head depth and encoder fine-tuning depth independently
affect seen performance and cross-attack robustness.

\textbf{Effect of head depth.} Comparing \texttt{ablation\_a} (single head,
freeze=4) against \texttt{ablation\_b} (deep head, freeze=4) at matched encoder
depth reveals that the deep head increases seen F1 by $+$0.72~pp
(0.9409$\rightarrow$0.9481) but raises RRD by $+$1.62~pp (1.51\%$\rightarrow$3.13\%),
exacting a cost of $-$0.83~pp in unseen F1 (0.9267$\rightarrow$0.9184). The deep
head extracts superior in-distribution signal at a marginal cost to cross-attack
transfer. For deployment scenarios where seen-set precision is paramount, the
deep head is preferable; for maximum generalisation margin, the shallow head
with partial freezing performs better.

\textbf{Effect of encoder fine-tuning depth.} Comparing \texttt{ablation\_b}
(deep head, freeze=4) against \texttt{ablation\_c} (deep head, freeze=0) at
matched head architecture demonstrates that full unfreezing raises seen ROC-AUC
by $+$0.15~pp (0.9908$\rightarrow$0.9923), raises unseen F1 by $+$0.79~pp
(0.9184$\rightarrow$0.9263), and lowers RRD by $-$0.92~pp (3.13\%$\rightarrow$2.21\%).
Full fine-tuning simultaneously improves in-distribution performance and
cross-attack robustness. This directly contradicts the over-fitting concern that
typically motivates partial freezing; given this dataset size and attack
taxonomy, the upper encoder layers actively benefit from full adaptation. The
sole trade-off is computational: unfreezing all six layers increases training
time by 1.66$\times$ relative to partial freezing (5,295~s versus 3,180~s).

\textbf{Joint recommendation.} \texttt{ablation\_c} (deep head, fully
unfrozen) serves as the best-performing configuration across every metric except
training efficiency. Conversely, \texttt{ablation\_b} (the officially proposed
model) achieves the best validation F1 alongside the best VRAM efficiency. It
completes training under AMP at 4,218.95~MB peak VRAM in 957~seconds, making
it the only configuration capable of practical AMP batch-128 execution on an
8~GB GPU.

\subsection{PDC Systems Insights}\label{ssec:pdc_insights}

The four PDC optimisations collectively reduce the preprocessing-to-inference
pipeline runtime from approximately 2.5~hours (cold download and sequential
processing) to under 9~seconds for preprocessing (TC1), approximately
16~minutes for AMP training (TC3), and 84~ms/batch for inference (TC4).
At the 120,000-sample scale, the dominant bottleneck shifts continuously
depending on the pipeline stage. CPU-bound parallel processing governs data
preparation (TC1); DataLoader overlap and memory bandwidth govern GPU feeding
(TC2); and the computation-to-memory-access ratio governs training and
inference throughput (TC3, TC4). Applying all four optimisations in
combination eliminates the dominant bottleneck at each stage to produce
additive performance gains.

The TC3 result carries particular significance for hardware democratisation.
AMP's FP16 activations reduce VRAM consumption from the hardware ceiling
($\sim$8~GB) to 4,218.95~MB. This enables a 4$\times$ batch increase that
reduces the total gradient-step count by approximately half, ultimately
yielding a 3.32$\times$ wall-clock speedup on a consumer GPU. Without AMP,
executing \texttt{batch\_size=128} remains physically infeasible on this
hardware. With AMP, the full experiment completes in under 16~minutes.

\subsection{Fast-DetectGPT Adversarial Fragility}\label{ssec:fastdetect}

The RAID adversarial results clarify a structural limitation inherent to
probability-based zero-shot detection. Under paraphrase attack, AUROC degrades
from 0.937 (clean) to approximately 0.789. While this represents a substantial
14.8~pp drop, the detector retains useful discriminative ability (AUC $\gg$ 0.5).
Under homoglyph attack, the degradation becomes severe: \texttt{gpt2-xl} drops
to AUROC 0.5257 (near-random), and \texttt{gpt-j-6B} falls to 0.6689. The 4-bit
NF4 quantization yields a 3.28$\times$ speed gain~\cite{dettmers2023} with no
measurable AUROC change on paraphrase data. This demonstrates that quantization
does not introduce additional adversarial fragility; the fragility is fundamental
to the probabilistic scoring signal itself.

The performance contrast between DistilBERT (F1 0.913--0.927 on paraphrase-type
unseen attacks) and Fast-DetectGPT (AUROC 0.787 on paraphrase RAID) remains
stark. Supervised training on a modest 120,000-sample corpus featuring
strategically diversified adversarial attack types achieves substantially
superior adversarial robustness at inference time compared to a state-of-the-art
zero-shot detector facing the identical attack class.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{ssec:limits}

\textbf{Unseen attack granularity.} The current unseen evaluation aggregates
all six unseen \texttt{data\_type} subsets (three paraphrase variants and
two prompt-adversarial variants) into a single F1 score. It remains unresolved
whether generalisation holds equally across \texttt{paraphrase\_dipper\_llm} and
\texttt{prompt\_sico}, which operate via distinct mechanisms despite both
belonging to the broader semantic-level category.

\textbf{Hardware scope.} All DistilBERT training results originate from a
single RTX~3050~8~GB GPU. Contemporary benchmarks like GREATER~\cite{li2025} and
Binoculars~\cite{hans2024} operate on enterprise hardware requiring 28--80~GB VRAM.
Direct empirical comparison on matched hardware falls outside the resource constraints
of this study.

\textbf{Inference model scale.} Kaggle resource limits restricted the primary
Fast-DetectGPT reproduction to \texttt{gpt-j-6B}, necessitating the exclusion of
\texttt{gpt-neox-20B}. Local adversarial runs successfully utilized both
\texttt{gpt-j-6B} and \texttt{gpt2-xl} to enable cross-scorer comparison, but this
did not replicate the full scoring model sweep present in the original publication.

\textbf{VRAM estimates.} Peak VRAM figures for the RAID NF4 runs serve as
estimates derived from published model-size parameters. Unreliable
\texttt{torch.cuda.reset\_peak\_memory\_stats()} behaviour between sequential
scorer loads prevented exact measurement. Actual peak VRAM may deviate from
these estimates by up to 10--15\%.

\subsection{Future Work}\label{ssec:future}

Several research directions follow directly from these results. Running separate
evaluations for each of the six unseen \texttt{data\_type} categories would reveal
whether generalisation holds uniformly or is driven by a single, highly
transferable attack subtype. Furthermore, replacing DistilBERT with BERT-base or
DeBERTa-v3-base would rigorously test whether the observed 1.11--3.13\% RRD
pattern remains specific to the distilled model scale or successfully generalises
to full-scale encoders. Finally, the severe homoglyph vulnerability (AUROC
0.526--0.669) motivates the construction of a domain-adaptive scoring model
trained specifically on Unicode-perturbed text to harden probabilistic pipelines.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We conducted a controlled empirical study evaluating cross-attack generalisation
in supervised AI text detection at the consumer-GPU scale. A DistilBERT binary
classifier, fine-tuned exclusively on character-level adversarial attack types,
achieves a Relative Robustness Degradation of 1.1--3.1\% on mechanistically distinct
unseen attacks. The model secures an unseen-split F1 between 0.913 and 0.927,
providing a definitive, positive validation of the cross-attack generalisation
hypothesis at the 66M parameter scale. Critically, this configuration does not
replicate the active-harm pattern observed in prior literature. The ablation
study demonstrates that full encoder fine-tuning simultaneously improves both
in-distribution performance and cross-attack robustness, whereas increased
classification head depth modestly trades generalisation for seen-set precision.

Four PDC optimisations facilitate this multi-configuration training workflow on
a single 8~GB consumer GPU. These include parallel preprocessing (yielding a
3.58$\times$ speedup), optimised DataLoader overlap, AMP training (yielding a
3.32$\times$ speedup and unlocking \texttt{batch\_size=128}), and AMP inference
mode (delivering a 54.25\% latency reduction). The auxiliary Fast-DetectGPT
study confirms implementation fidelity (reproducing an AUROC of 0.9370 against a
0.9338 target) and establishes that 4-bit NF4 quantization does not compound
adversarial fragility. Instead, the fragility remains structurally inherent to
the probabilistic detection signal itself.

These findings carry immediate practical significance. An adversarially augmented
DistilBERT model at 66M parameters, trained on standard consumer hardware in
under 16~minutes, substantially outperforms a state-of-the-art zero-shot
probabilistic detector under identical adversarial attack conditions.
